This week Washington's crypto bill collapsed, Bitcoin didn't care, and everyone from Visa to the ECB found new ways to remind the industry that adults are still in the room.

The Clarity Act Died in the Senate — So the SEC and CFTC Grabbed the Wheel

The Clarity Act failed to advance in the Senate, and rather than waiting around for Congress to try again, both the SEC and CFTC immediately started moving on their own crypto rulemaking agendas, effectively filling the vacuum Congress left behind.

This is the story of the week disguised as a footnote: legislation dies, but the regulatory machinery doesn't skip a beat. Agencies writing their own rules without a statute to lean on is exactly the kind of ambiguity crypto has spent a decade complaining about, so watching them race to fill that gap is either reassuring or terrifying depending on how much you trust unelected rulemakers over gridlocked lawmakers.

Bitcoin Shrugs Off the Clarity Act Flop, Rips Back Past $80,000

Bitcoin reclaimed $80,000 with Solana and Hyperliquid rallying alongside it, as markets apparently decided the SEC and CFTC pushing ahead with rulemaking was reassuring enough to offset the Clarity Act stalling out in the Senate.

There's something almost comedic about a market that spent weeks bracing for the Clarity Act as a make-or-break moment, only to shrug it off entirely once the bill actually failed. It's a reminder that legislative theater and price action live in two different universes, and the latter increasingly doesn't wait for permission from the former.

Grayscale's Zcash ETF Is So Popular It's Splitting Its Shares

Grayscale's Zcash ETF is doing a 3-for-1 forward share split after pulling in more than $233 million in under a month, making it more affordable for retail investors to buy in as Wall Street piles into what's being called crypto's hottest privacy trade.

Privacy coins spent years as the industry's awkward, semi-banned cousin, so watching a Zcash product get Wall Street's classic 'let's make it look cheaper' treatment is a genuinely funny plot twist. Whether that enthusiasm is about the tech, the narrative, or just chasing a hot chart is a question worth sitting with.

Visa Finally Closes the 'Buy Meme Coins, Earn Airline Points' Loophole

After banks lost their push for tighter stablecoin rules in the failed Clarity Act, JPMorgan scored a narrower win as Visa moves to stop meme coin purchases from being coded as ordinary 'digital media' purchases, which had been letting buyers rack up card rewards on their degen trades.

It's a small fix to a genuinely absurd loophole — getting airline miles for buying a dog-themed token was never going to survive contact with a bank's lawyers. But it's also a good example of how the rules of crypto get written one obscure merchant-category-code fight at a time, far from any congressional hearing.

North Korean Fake Job Recruiters Infected 30,000 Devices, Stole $10.7M

The North Korean cyber group WaterPlum targeted developers with fake job offers at crypto, AI, and NFT companies, infecting tens of thousands of devices and making off with $10.7 million in crypto in the process.

State-sponsored actors posing as recruiters to get inside developers' machines is one of those threats that sounds like fiction until it's a recurring headline. If you're a builder in this space, the job offer in your DMs is now officially a category of risk alongside phishing links and rug pulls.

Read more at Cointelegraph →

Polymarket Dodged a $10M Fraud Attempt While Its CEO Pushed Growth Over Compliance

Per the Wall Street Journal, Polymarket faced a $10 million fraud attempt as its CEO reportedly prioritized growth over compliance concerns, and in a separate attack hackers compromised nearly 500 user accounts using stolen personal information.

This is the tension every fast-growing platform eventually has to answer for: move fast and hope compliance catches up, or slow down and risk losing the market to someone who won't. Polymarket clearly chose speed, and this week's headline is what that choice can look like from the outside.

Read more at The Block →

ECB's Lagarde Reportedly Intervened to Block Binance's EU License

The Wall Street Journal reported that ECB President Christine Lagarde personally intervened to block Binance from obtaining its MiCA license in the EU, according to CoinDesk.

MiCA was supposed to be Europe's answer to regulatory clarity — a rulebook everyone could follow to get licensed and move on. If the head of the ECB is instead making case-by-case calls on who gets in, that's a pretty loud signal that 'clarity' in crypto regulation is relative no matter which continent you're on.
